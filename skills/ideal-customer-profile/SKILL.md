## Purpose

Define an Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) that helps the team answer:

- which customers are most worth targeting
- which customers are most likely to buy
- which customers are most likely to succeed after adoption
- which customers should not be prioritized yet

This is not a persona. It is a customer selection tool.

## Key Concepts

### ICP Is About Customer Fit, Not User Description

A persona describes a user. An ICP describes the kind of customer account or organization that is the best match for the product or solution.

An ICP usually includes factors like:

- company type
- team structure
- workflow complexity
- buying trigger
- implementation readiness
- value realization conditions

If you only describe "the team lead" or "the operations manager," you still do not have an ICP.

### Good ICPs Combine Four Realities

Strong customer selection requires all four:

1. **Problem intensity** — does this customer feel the problem sharply?
2. **Buying reality** — can this customer actually prioritize and purchase the solution?
3. **Implementation readiness** — can this customer adopt and operationalize it?
4. **Value realization** — is the customer likely to see meaningful outcomes after using it?

Many teams over-focus on problem intensity and ignore the other three.

### Best-Fit Is Not the Same as Largest Market

The biggest market is not always the best starting point.

An ICP is not "everyone who could theoretically use this." It is the narrower slice where:

- the pain is real
- the value is clear
- the sale is plausible
- the rollout has a real chance to succeed

### ICP Is a Choice Tool

The ICP should help with decisions like:

- which pipeline opportunities deserve attention
- which product requests matter more
- which references or case studies to pursue
- which launch story or solution packaging should come first

If the ICP cannot influence choices, it is too vague.

### Why This Works

- sharpens product and GTM focus
- reduces broad, low-conversion targeting
- improves solution packaging and qualification
- makes customer selection explicit instead of tribal

### Anti-Patterns

- **User-only profile** — describes a role but not the customer environment
- **Firmographics only** — company size and industry without problem or readiness
- **Everyone-with-pain ICP** — includes every customer who can say "yes, that's hard"
- **Revenue fantasy ICP** — picks whales with low fit because they are large logos
- **No negative boundary** — never states who should be deprioritized

### When to Use This

- Before packaging a new B2B or SaaS solution
- During GTM planning
- When product and sales disagree on who matters most
- When early demand is broad but win-rates or adoption are weak

### When NOT to Use This

- For purely consumer products with no account-level buying dynamic
- When the underlying customer problem is still unclear
- As a replacement for user personas or JTBD work

## Application

Use `template.md` for the final artifact.

### Step 1: Start with the Problem and Solution Context

Gather:

- problem statement
- target user or persona
- product or solution narrative
- current demand signals
- sales or CS observations
- rollout constraints

If the problem is still vague, the ICP will become circular and generic.

### Step 2: Define the Customer Environment

Describe the account-level context:

- company or organization type
- team structure
- collaboration shape
- workflow or execution complexity
- operational maturity

This is where you move beyond user role language.

### Step 3: Identify Fit Dimensions

For this solution, define the dimensions that truly predict fit.

Common examples:

- urgency of the problem
- number of collaborators involved
- process maturity
- existing workaround pain
- buying owner clarity
- willingness to run a pilot
- ability to maintain the required operating discipline

Do not copy generic ICP dimensions if they do not affect success for this product.

### Step 4: Distinguish Strong Fit, Medium Fit, and Poor Fit

State:

- which customers are strong-fit
- which customers are plausible but not ideal
- which customers should be deprioritized for now

This is the most important section. If everything looks like a fit, the ICP has failed.

### Step 5: Define Triggers, Risks, and Disqualifiers

A strong ICP includes:

- buying or urgency triggers
- implementation prerequisites
- common disqualifiers
- warning signs that a sale may look good but fail later

This is what makes the artifact useful to product, sales, and customer success at the same time.

### Step 6: Turn the ICP into Action

End with:

- qualification cues
- messaging implications
- product implications
- rollout implications

If the artifact does not change how teams qualify, message, or prioritize, it is still too abstract.

### Example of a Good ICP Statement

"The best-fit customers are multi-owner enterprise teams running execution-heavy projects where the project lead still relies on meetings, chat follow-up, or spreadsheets to understand status, and where there is enough task discipline to make risk visibility useful."

Why this works:

- defines the customer environment
- names the operational pain
- includes an implementation readiness condition
- excludes customers who would create false-positive demand

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing buyer title with customer fit
- Writing an ICP that could apply to half the market
- Ignoring rollout readiness and overvaluing logo size
- Treating every customer request as evidence of ICP fit
- Skipping explicit non-fit conditions
